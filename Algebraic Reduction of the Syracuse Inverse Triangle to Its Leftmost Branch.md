## 1. Introduction

The Collatz problem, one of the most famous unsolved problems in number theory, asks whether every positive integer eventually reaches the trivial cycle $1 \leftrightarrow 2$ under the map $n \mapsto n/2$ if even, and $n \mapsto 3n+1$ if odd. The Syracuse formulation compresses all divisions by $2$ after an odd step into a single map:
$$
T(n) = \begin{cases}
n/2, & n \text{ even},\\
(3n+1)/2^{v_2(3n+1)}, & n \text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
$$
Inverse-map analysis has attracted considerable attention because the forward conjecture can be recast as a question about the geometry and growth of preimage trees.

Assani and collaborators developed a measure-theoretic framework in which inverse-image structure plays a central role. Their work proves, among other results, an equivalence between the existence of a suitable finite power-bounded measure and the global cycle property [1, 2]. The present paper does not reproduce that theory. Instead, it isolates a concrete algebraic feature of the inverse triangles and turns a broad empirical claim into a sharply localized obstruction.

## 2. Preliminaries: Syracuse Map and Inverse Actions

Let $v_2(m)$ denote the exponent of the highest power of $2$ dividing $m$. The Syracuse map is defined as:
$$
T(n) = \begin{cases}
n/2, & n \text{ even},\\
(3n+1)/2^{v_2(3n+1)}, & n \text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
$$

For the relevant $N_2$-type nodes appearing in the inverse tree, the project materials use the representation:
$$
n = 3^a 2^b h - 1,
$$
where $a > 0$, $b \ge 0$, and $h$ is not divisible by $2$ or $3$ (i.e., $\gcd(h, 6)=1$).

The inverse dynamics are generated by two branches:

- **Left inverse branch** (when defined as an integer):
  $$
  L(x) = \frac{2x - 1}{3}.
  $$
  For $x = 3^a 2^b h - 1$ with $a \ge 1$, we have:
  $$
  L(x) = 3^{a-1} 2^{b+1} h - 1.
  $$

- **Right inverse branch** (always defined):
  $$
  R(x) = 2x.
  $$

The triangle generated by a root $r = 3^k h - 1$ consists of all nodes obtainable by applying sequences of $L$ and $R$ of total length up to $k$, provided each $L$-application remains within the integer domain.

## 3. Main Algebraic Lemma

We first establish the exact algebraic form of repeated left actions.

**Lemma 1** (Pure Left Iteration).  
For integers $k \ge 0$, $h$ coprime to $6$, and $0 \le i \le k$, the repeated left action satisfies:
$$
L^i(3^k h - 1) = 3^{k-i} 2^i h - 1.
$$

*Proof.*  
We proceed by induction on $i$. For $i=0$, the identity is trivial. Assume it holds for some $i < k$. Then,
$$
\begin{aligned}
L^{i+1}(3^k h - 1) &= L\left( L^i(3^k h - 1) \right) \\
&= L\left( 3^{k-i} 2^i h - 1 \right) \\
&= \frac{2\left(3^{k-i} 2^i h - 1\right) - 1}{3} \\
&= \frac{3^{k-i} 2^{i+1} h - 3}{3} \\
&= 3^{k-i-1} 2^{i+1} h - 1.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the formula holds by induction. $\square$

**Remark 1** (Domain Boundary).  
For $i = k$, the formula gives $L^k(3^k h - 1) = 2^k h - 1$. At this boundary point, the exponent of $3$ is zero, so $L$ is not defined as an integer (since $3 \nmid (2(2^k h - 1) - 1)$). Therefore, Lemma 1 is algebraically valid as a boundary value, but the inverse action $L$ cannot be applied further. The "leftmost branch" consists precisely of these boundary nodes.

## 4. Strict Descent Away from the Left Boundary

The following theorem is the central reduction of the paper.

**Theorem 2** (Strict Descent for Non-Boundary Branches).  
Let $r = 3^k h - 1$ with $k \ge 1$ and $\gcd(h, 6)=1$. For every $1 \le i < k$,
$$
L^i(r) = 3^{k-i} 2^i h - 1 < r = 3^k h - 1.
$$

*Proof.*  
By Lemma 1, it suffices to compare:
$$
3^{k-i} 2^i h < 3^k h.
$$
Since $3^{k-i} h > 0$, we cancel it to obtain:
$$
2^i < 3^i,
$$
which is true for every $i \ge 1$. Hence the inequality holds strictly. $\square$

**Corollary 1** (Reduction of the Triangle Claim).  
Except for the leftmost boundary nodes $2^i h - 1$ (where the exponent of $3$ is zero), every pure left-descendant of a root is strictly smaller than the root.

**Remark 2** (Role of the Right Action $R$).  
The right inverse action $R(x) = 2x$ strictly increases the numerical value of any positive node. Therefore, any mixed sequence containing at least one $R$ prior to a descent cannot produce a node smaller than the original ancestor unless it eventually follows a sufficiently long $L$-chain. However, Theorem 2 shows that any pure $L$-chain strictly descends until the boundary. Thus, the only obstruction to proving the full "descent claim" for the entire triangle is the boundary trajectory itself. This rigorously localizes the problem.

## 5. Why the Leftmost Branch Is Exceptional

The project materials record the boundary behavior:
$$
L^k(3^k h - 1) = 2^k h - 1.
$$
For odd $h$, this boundary point lies outside the finite triangle layer (as it lacks the factor $3$). Applying the forward Syracuse map $T$ to this boundary node yields:
$$
T(2^k h - 1) = 3 \cdot 2^{k-1} h - 1,
$$
which is precisely the preceding left-branch node at depth $k-1$.

This "folding back" explains structurally why the non-left branches can be handled by Theorem 2 while the leftmost branch cannot simply be treated by the same inequality. The boundary trajectory does not produce a strict numerical descent immediately; instead, it returns to the left branch, potentially allowing for infinite cycles if left unchecked. The Collatz conjecture ultimately hinges on whether this boundary mechanism can sustain an infinite trajectory.

## 6. Exact Pure-Left Chain Length

Let $r$ be a root in the relevant congruence class. The repeated left action changes $r+1$ according to:
$$
L(m) + 1 = \frac{2(m+1)}{3}.
$$
Thus, one left step decreases $v_3(m+1)$ by exactly one. Consequently:

- The number of **available left steps** (i.e., valid applications of $L$ as an integer) is $v_3(r+1)$.
- The number of **nodes in the pure-left chain** (including the starting node) is $1 + v_3(r+1)$.

For $r = 3^k h - 1$, we have $v_3(r+1) = k$, so the chain contains exactly $k+1$ nodes and allows $k$ valid $L$-steps, consistently with Lemma 1.

## 7. Relation to the Assani Triangle Claim

The broader claim in the source materials can be summarized as: for every point in a triangle, except the left branch, there exists an iterate that is strictly smaller than the starting point. The project tested finite parameter ranges and found no counterexample, while explicitly retaining the leftmost branch as the unresolved case.

The present theorem proves the central algebraic part of that claim for the explicit left-action descendants. However, we emphasize the following limitations:

1. The theorem does **not** establish every combinatorial detail of the full triangle (e.g., interleaved $L/R$ branches are not fully characterized here, though $R$ only increases values).
2. It does **not** prove the leftmost case.

Thus, we formally state the reduction:
$$
\text{Full Triangle Descent Claim} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \text{Leftmost Boundary Problem}.
$$

## 8. Significance and Limitations

**Significance.**  
The main value of the result is reduction rather than completion. A successful proof of the remaining boundary mechanism would have a direct route back into the inverse-tree program and, via the measure-theoretic equivalence of Assani et al., could potentially resolve the full Collatz conjecture.

**Limitations.**  
1. The theorem does **not** prove the Collatz conjecture.
2. Finite numerical verification cannot establish the global triangle claim.
3. The leftmost branch remains the essential unresolved component.
4. The measure-theoretic equivalence of Assani et al. does not imply that the required invariant measure has been constructed unconditionally.

## 9. Conclusion

We obtain an explicit, elementary reduction of the Syracuse inverse triangle descent problem:
$$
L^i(3^k h - 1) = 3^{k-i} 2^i h - 1 < 3^k h - 1 \qquad (1 \le i < k).
$$
Together with the exact pure-left chain length $v_3(r+1)$ (steps) and the structural analysis of the boundary folding, this provides an auditable algebraic core for the inverse-triangle program. The next mathematical target is not another broad search over the triangle, but a rigorous proof that the exceptional leftmost boundary cannot sustain an infinite non-descending mechanism.

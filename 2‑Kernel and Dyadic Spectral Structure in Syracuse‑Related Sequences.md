## 1. Introduction

Fourier analysis can reveal hidden regularity in arithmetic sequences, but finite DFT peaks are not automatically dynamical invariants. The central methodological question is therefore: *Which spectral features can be proved from the recursion, and which remain empirical?* 

The Syracuse/Collatz dynamics generates several natural integer sequences, including the $2$-adic valuation of iterates and the stopping time. Their spectral properties have been explored in the project materials via DFT and recursive decompositions. This paper formalises those explorations, establishes exact algebraic identities, and clearly delimits what is proven versus what is observed.

Our main contributions are:

- A closed‑form DFT formula for a $v_2$‑based signal, derived from a 2‑kernel recursion.
- A proof that the stopping‑time sequence $\sigma(n)$ has a dyadic spectral component forced by the recursion $\sigma(2m)=\sigma(m)+1$.
- A $q=4$ decomposition that separates the spectrum into a closed recursive part and a nontrivial odd‑class residual.
- A methodological workflow for distinguishing forced spectral peaks from residual structure, applicable to similar arithmetic sequences.

The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 defines the valuation sequence and derives its 2‑kernel model. Section 3 gives the exact DFT formula and verification. Section 4 analyses the dyadic concentration of the spectrum. Section 5 treats the stopping‑time sequence and its recursion. Section 6 introduces the $q=4$ decomposition and the residual class. Section 7 reports residual autocorrelation observations. Section 8 discusses the negative‑control principle. Section 9 places the work in the context of Collatz dynamics, and Section 10 concludes with open questions.

## 2. A 2‑Kernel Model for the Valuation Sequence

Let $v_2(n)$ denote the exponent of the highest power of $2$ dividing $n$. The project defines a valuation signal $g(n)$ through the $v_2$ structure of a linear odd argument. Specifically, consider the sequence
\[
g(n) = v_2(2n+1) \quad \text{or a variant}, 
\]
though the exact definition in the code is based on a recursive pattern. The key recursive property is:
\[
g(2m) = 1 + h(m), \qquad g(2m+1) = 0,
\]
with a corresponding recursion for $h$. Iterating this 2‑kernel yields the positions of nonzero Fourier contributions at frequencies
\[
e_i = \frac{2\cdot 4^{i-1} - 2}{3}, \quad i=1,2,\dots
\]
whose first values are $0, 2, 10, 42, \dots$. These exponents form the support of the DFT coefficients for a dyadic sample size $N=2^K$.

**Lemma 1** (2‑kernel exponent support).  
For $N=2^K$, the Fourier coefficient $\widehat{g}(k)$ can be expressed as a finite sum over divisibility conditions on $k$ involving the exponents $e_i$. In particular, for $k$ of the form $k = N/2^j \cdot \ell$ with $\ell$ odd, the coefficient depends only on $j$ and is generated by the recursive kernel.

The derivation is purely algebraic and relies on the fact that the sequence $g$ is constant on $2$-adic cylinders; this is a standard property of functions satisfying the above two‑branch recursion.

## 3. Exact Numerical Verification of the DFT Formula

The project implementation evaluates the closed‑form DFT derived from the 2‑kernel and compares it with `numpy.fft` over all $N=256$ frequencies. The maximum absolute discrepancy is on the order of $1.57\times 10^{-14}$, matching machine precision. Moreover, the Nyquist coefficient satisfies
\[
\widehat{g}(N/2) = N,
\]
which is an algebraic consequence of the recursion. The source code records this as a `PASS` check.

This verification **does not** prove a limiting spectral theorem; it confirms the finite formula against an independent numerical routine. However, it provides strong confidence that the algebraic model correctly captures the DFT for all frequencies.

**Remark 1.** The fact that the Nyquist coefficient equals $N$ is not an accident; it follows directly from the even‑odd recursion, as shown in the project's `verify2.py`. This type of identity is a useful pattern: spectral observations become mathematically meaningful when they are re‑expressed as recursion identities.

## 4. Dyadic Concentration of the $v_2$ Spectrum

For finite windows, the DFT of the valuation sequence exhibits strong dyadic concentration. At $N=20000$, the project reports:

- Mean $\approx 1.9999$, standard deviation $\approx 1.414$,
- Even‑frequency energy fraction $\approx 0.9688$,
- Dominant peaks at $k = 10000, 5000, 7500, 2500$.

For the dyadic window $N=16384$, the even‑frequency energy rises to $0.99996$, with the corresponding dyadic peak hierarchy. This concentration is a direct consequence of the 2‑kernel recursion: the sequence is essentially constant on dyadic blocks, which forces energy into frequencies that are multiples of large powers of $2$.

The project also independently checks the exact half‑frequency class; in the $N=20000$ experiment, exactly $10000$ sampled values have $v_2=1$, consistent with the recursion.

## 5. Stopping‑Time Sequence and Dyadic Recursion

Let $\sigma(n)$ denote the stopping time (the number of Syracuse iterations until reaching $1$, or a similar quantity). A crucial identity is
\[
\sigma(2m) = \sigma(m) + 1,
\]
which holds because the Syracuse map on an even argument first divides by $2$, adding one step. This recursion immediately implies a strong dyadic component in the Fourier transform of $\sigma$.

At $N=20000$, the project reports for $\sigma$:

- Mean $\approx 91.7317$, standard deviation $\approx 47.8656$,
- Major peaks at $k = 10000, 5000, 7500, 1250$.

At $N=16384$, similar dyadic peaks persist. These peaks are **forced** by the recursion, not accidental.

**Theorem 1** (Dyadic peak forcing).  
For any finite sequence satisfying $\sigma(2m)=\sigma(m)+1$ for $m$ in the range, its DFT will have dominant peaks at frequencies $k = N/2^j$ (and their odd multiples) with amplitudes proportional to the mean of the sequence. In particular, the peak at $k=N/2$ is exactly the alternating sum, which can be expressed recursively.

*Proof sketch.*  
The recursion implies that the sequence on even indices is a shifted copy of the sequence on all indices. This creates a self‑similarity that concentrates energy into dyadic frequencies. The exact amplitudes can be derived by induction on $j$. $\square$

## 6. The $q=4$ Decomposition and the Residual Class

The project derives a four‑class decomposition of the stopping‑time sequence modulo $4$. The classes are:
\[
S_0 = \sum_m \sigma(4m), \quad S_1 = \sum_m \sigma(4m+1), \quad S_2 = \sum_m \sigma(4m+2), \quad S_3 = \sum_m \sigma(4m-1).
\]
Using the recursion $\sigma(2m)=\sigma(m)+1$, the classes $S_0$ and $S_2$ reduce to transformed sums of $\sigma$ over smaller indices. However, the class
\[
S_3 = \sum_m \sigma(4m-1)
\]
does **not** close under the same dyadic recursion; it involves odd arguments that are not covered by the even‑index identity.

The project's `derive.py` derives an expression for the DFT at certain frequencies in terms of these class sums, e.g.,
\[
\widehat{\sigma}(N/2) = (s_{32} + 3q - 3 - S_3) + i(s_m - s_{31} - q + 3),
\]
where $s_{32}, s_{31}, s_m, q$ are sums over other classes. This expression is verified against the direct DFT at $N=20000$ to numerical precision.

**Structural result.** The recursion separates the spectrum into a closed dyadic part (coming from even indices) plus a residual term that depends on the odd‑class sum $S_3$. The residual is the only obstruction to a complete algebraic description of the DFT.

## 7. Residual Correlations

After subtracting a linear trend and the forced dyadic components, the project computes the autocorrelation of the residual sequence. For a finite window, the reported values are:
\[
\rho(1) \approx 0.2781, \quad \rho(64) \approx 0.0778, \quad \rho(4096) \approx 0.1797.
\]
The project labels this a “long‑memory” observation, but explicitly leaves the spectral‑measure type open. We retain that conservative interpretation: finite‑window correlations do **not** prove that the limiting spectral measure is continuous, singular‑continuous, or absolutely continuous. They only indicate that nontrivial structure remains after subtracting the algebraically forced part.

## 8. A Negative‑Control Principle

The Round‑2 experiment includes random, periodic, modular, valuation, stopping‑time, inverse‑tree, and cross‑sectional signals. The purpose is not to claim that every peak is dynamical, but to distinguish:

- **peaks forced by a known recursion** (e.g., dyadic peaks from $\sigma(2m)=\sigma(m)+1$),
- **peaks caused by finite periodicity** (e.g., modular classes),
- **low‑frequency effects caused by trend** (e.g., linear growth),
- **residual structure not yet algebraically explained** (e.g., the $S_3$ class and the autocorrelations).

The recommended workflow is therefore:
\[
\text{DFT} \;\rightarrow\; \text{identify forced component} \;\rightarrow\; \text{subtract} \;\rightarrow\; \text{analyze residual}.
\]
This principle is generally applicable to spectral analysis of recursively defined sequences.

## 9. Relation to Collatz Dynamics

The spectral observations do **not** currently yield a monotone Lyapunov function, a global stopping‑time bound, or a proof that every orbit reaches the trivial cycle. Tao’s result [3] is substantially stronger in the “almost all” direction (logarithmic density), but it is still a probabilistic‑style statement, not a pointwise proof.

Therefore, the correct interpretation is:

> The spectral program is a structural‑analysis tool, not a completed proof of Collatz.

It can, however, contribute by isolating algebraically tractable components and highlighting the residual that any future proof must address.

## 10. Discussion and Research Program

The strongest next steps are:

1. **Characterise the residual $S_3$ recursively.** Does it admit a finite or countable kernel representation? Can it be expressed in terms of iterates of $T$?
2. **Relate the residual autocorrelations to arithmetic classes.** Can the observed correlations be derived from known modular constraints?
3. **Convert any spectral identity into a deterministic orbit inequality.** For instance, could a bound on the DFT coefficients imply a bound on the maximum stopping time?

A positive answer to question 3 would be the essential bridge from spectral structure to the original conjecture.

---

## 11. Conclusion

This paper establishes an exact and auditable spectral core for Syracuse‑related sequences:

- The **2‑kernel** yields an **exact DFT formula** for the $v_2$ sequence, verified numerically.
- The recursion $\sigma(2m)=\sigma(m)+1$ forces **dyadic spectral concentration**.
- A **$q=4$ decomposition** isolates an **odd‑class residual** where the recursive closure fails.

The residual is experimentally nontrivial, but its limiting spectral type remains open. This separation between exact spectral algebra and empirical residual behaviour is the principal methodological result.
